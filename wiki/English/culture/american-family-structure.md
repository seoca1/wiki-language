# American Family Structure

**Overview:** American family structure has shifted from large multigenerational households to smaller nuclear families, with rising single-parent households and delayed marriage. Today, "immediate family" usually means parents and siblings, while "extended family" covers grandparents, aunts, uncles, and cousins, a much narrower kinship circle than in many other cultures.

## Key Points

- **Nuclear family**: The dominant model (parents + children). Historically idealized in 1950s postwar suburbia. Today roughly 70% of US households are non-family or single-person, and only about 20% fit the classic nuclear-family pattern.
- **Single-parent households**: Common; about 23% of children under 18 live with one parent (predominantly mothers). Public assistance, child support, and stigma vary by state.
- **Multigenerational households**: Growing again, especially among Hispanic, Asian-American, and Black families, and increasingly due to elder care and student debt. Pew estimates ~1 in 5 Americans now lives in a multigenerational home.
- **Family dinner**: Often cited as a cultural ideal ("family dinner every night"), but surveys show most families eat together only 3-5 times a week, with TV or phones at the table.
- **"Immediate family"**: Parents, siblings, spouse, and children. Spouse and children are often listed first in obituaries and airline bereavement policies.
- **"Extended family"**: Grandparents, aunts, uncles, cousins. American English uses one word "cousin" for all first cousins, unlike Korean (사촌) or Chinese (堂/表兄弟姐妹), which distinguish maternal vs. paternal and older vs. younger.
- **Independence from family**: Adult children are expected to leave home at 18, pay their own way, and build a separate life. Living with parents past college age is increasingly common but still carries mild stigma.
- **"Family values"**: A political phrase (1980s+) referring to two-parent households, traditional gender roles, and church attendance. Interpretations vary widely.

## Cross-language Connections

- **Coreano**: 한국 가족은 3-4 세대 동거가 더 일반적 (부모 + 자녀 + 손주). 호칭 체계 (오빠/형/언니/누나/삼촌/고모 등) 가 매우 정교. 미국은 immediate family 만 좁게 부른다.
- **Japonés**: 일본 核家族 (kakukazoku) 모델이 1950년대부터 표준화, 미국 핵가족과 시기·구조 유사. 일본 親戚 (shinseki) 도 넓은 친척 네트워크지만 호칭은 미국보다 정교 (叔父/伯父 vs uncle).
- **Chino**: 중국 가족은 大家庭 (dà jiātíng) 전통 + 计划生育 (one-child policy) 영향. 미국보다 부모 부양 의무가 강하다. 영어의 단일 단어 "uncle" 은 중국어 叔叔/舅舅/伯伯/姑父 로 분리.
- **Español**: 히스패닉 미국 가정은 multigenerational 비율이 평균보다 높음. 스페인 어휘 tío/tía/primo 도 모든 uncle/aunt/cousin 통합, 미국과 동일. 영미권의 "cousin once removed" 같은 구분은 없음.

## Examples

«In the US, "immediate family" usually means parents, siblings, and children. Anything beyond that, like grandparents or cousins, is "extended family."» (미국에서 "immediate family" 는 보통 부모, 형제, 자녀를 뜻한다. 그 너머 조부모나 사촌은 모두 "extended family" 다.)

«My parents are divorced, so I grew up in a single-parent household with my mom and my younger sister.» (우리 부모가 이혼해서 나는 엄마와 여동생과 함께 한부모 가정에서 자랐다.)

«After college, most Americans are expected to move out and live on their own, even if they can't afford to.» (대졸 후 대부분의 미국인은 재정 상황이 어렵더라도 독립해서 사는 것이 기대된다.)
