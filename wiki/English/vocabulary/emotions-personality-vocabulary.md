## Basic Emotions

### happy

**Part of Speech:** adjective

**Definition:** feeling or showing pleasure or contentment

**IPA / Pronunciation:** /ˈhæpi/

**Etymology:** Middle English *hap* "chance, luck" + *-y* → "fortunate, lucky" → "pleased". Cognate with *happen*, *haphazard*, *perhaps*.

#### Examples

- She looks **happy** today. — Adjective: mood
- A **happy** childhood. — Adjective: fortunate
- **Happy** birthday! — Formulaic greeting

#### Related Terms

- ''joy'' — Intense happiness
- ''glad'' — Pleased about specific
- ''cheerful'' — Visibly happy

#### Cultural Notes

"Happy" is the default positive emotion word in English — lower intensity than "joy", "delight", "ecstasy". "Happy" in greetings (Happy Birthday, Happy Holidays) is performative politeness. "Not happy" = British understatement for angry/displeased.

### sad

**Part of Speech:** adjective

**Definition:** feeling or showing sorrow; unhappy

**IPA / Pronunciation:** /sæd/

**Etymology:** Old English *sæd* "sated, weary, heavy" → "grave, serious" → "sorrowful". Cognate with German *satt* "full, sated", Latin *satis* "enough".

#### Examples

- He felt **sad** after the movie. — Adjective: emotion
- A **sad** story. — Adjective: causing sadness
- **Sad** to say, it failed. — Adjective: regrettable

#### Related Terms

- ''sorrow'' — Deep sadness
- ''unhappy'' — Not happy
- ''depressed'' — Clinically/very sad

#### Cultural Notes

"Sad" is neutral register. "I'm sad" = direct statement. British "a bit sad" = understatement for very sad. "Sad!" as standalone exclamation = dismissive judgment.

### angry

**Part of Speech:** adjective

**Definition:** feeling or showing strong annoyance, displeasure, or hostility

**IPA / Pronunciation:** /ˈæŋɡri/

**Etymology:** Old Norse *angr* "grief, sorrow" → Old English *ange* "painful, distressing" → "angered". Related to *anger* (noun), *angst* (German loan).

#### Examples

- She was **angry** at the delay. — Adjective: emotion
- An **angry** letter. — Adjective: expressing anger
- **Angry** customers demanded refunds. — Adjective: describing group

#### Related Terms

- ''furious'' — Extremely angry
- ''annoyed'' — Mildly angry
- ''irritated'' — Impatient anger

#### Cultural Notes

Direct anger expression is more acceptable in US/UK than in East Asian cultures. "I'm angry" = assertive. "I'm not angry, just disappointed" = passive-aggressive classic. Workplace: "frustrated" or "concerned" preferred over "angry".

### afraid

**Part of Speech:** adjective (predicative only: *I am afraid*, not *an afraid child*)

**Definition:** feeling fear or anxiety; frightened

**IPA / Pronunciation:** /əˈfreɪd/

**Etymology:** Old French *affrayer* "to frighten" → *esfrei* "frightened" → Latin *exfridare* "to break peace" (*ex-* + *fridus* "peace"). Cognate with *affray*.

#### Examples

- I'm **afraid** of spiders. — Predicative adjective
- **Afraid** so / **afraid** not. — Formulaic: polite yes/no
- She looked **afraid**. — Predicative after linking verb

#### Related Terms

- ''scared'' — Attributive OK: a scared child
- ''frightened'' — More formal
- ''terrified'' — Extreme fear

#### Cultural Notes

"Afraid" cannot precede nouns (*afraid child* ✗ → *frightened child* ✓). "I'm afraid..." = polite hedge for bad news ("I'm afraid we're full"). "Afraid of one's shadow" = easily frightened.

## Positive Personality Traits

### kind

**Part of Speech:** adjective

**Definition:** friendly, generous, considerate; showing empathy

**IPA / Pronunciation:** /kaɪnd/

**Etymology:** Old English *gecynde* "natural, native, innate" → *cynd* "nature, race" → "born with, natural disposition" → "benevolent". Cognate with *kin*, *kind* (noun: type).

#### Examples

- She's a **kind** person. — Attributive
- **Kind** of you to help. — Predicative (evaluative)
- **Kind** regards. — Formulaic closing

#### Related Terms

- ''kindness'' — Noun
- ''generous'' — Giving freely
- ''compassionate'' — Feeling with

#### Cultural Notes

"Kind" is the baseline positive trait in English — expected, not exceptional. "Kind of" = hedge ("kind of tired"). "Kind regards" = standard professional closing. "Kill them with kindness" = strategic niceness.
