# complaints (English) expressions

> **Theme:** Registering complaints (polite to direct)
> **Level:** B1-C1

**Overview:** English complaints scale from polite service-recovery phrases ("I'd like to speak with the manager") to direct consumer-rights statements ("I want a refund"). Register matters: indirect complaints preserve relationships; direct complaints are appropriate for clear breaches (defective goods, billing errors).

---

## I'd like to speak with the manager, please

**Literal Translation:** "I want to talk with the manager, please"

**Meaning:** Polite escalation phrase. The default next step when frontline staff cannot resolve a complaint.

**Usage Context:** Polite / formal. Restaurants, hotels, retail. Add "please" for extra polish; dropping "please" sounds terse.

**Pattern:** I'd like to + speak with + the manager + please.

### Examples

- I'd like to speak with the manager, please. — Polite escalation
- Could I speak with the manager? — Slightly softer
- Get me the manager. — Direct / aggressive

### Cultural Background

In US/UK service culture, asking for "the manager" is a normal escalation, not aggression. Frontline staff expect it. The phrase "I'd like to" softens it; "Get me" is the same intent but signals anger.

## This isn't what I ordered

**Literal Translation:** Same.

**Meaning:** Direct statement that the received item differs from the order. Standard restaurant/retail complaint opener.

**Usage Context:** Polite to neutral. Restaurants, retail, online orders. Pair with showing evidence (receipt, photo) for fastest resolution.

**Pattern:** This + isn't + what + (subject) + ordered.

### Examples

- This isn't what I ordered. I asked for the chicken, not the fish. — Restaurant
- This isn't what I ordered online. — E-commerce
- This isn't what I ordered, can I get a refund? — With explicit ask

### Cultural Background

In English complaint discourse, stating the gap between expectation and reality is the first move. Followed by the desired resolution (refund, exchange, discount).

- [[complaints]] — There's a problem with my order.

## I'm not satisfied with the service

**Literal Translation:** Same.

**Meaning:** Formal statement of dissatisfaction. Used for serious complaints where you want documentation.

**Usage Context:** Formal. Customer service hotlines, complaint letters, formal feedback.

**Pattern:** I'm not satisfied with + the service.

### Examples

- I'm not satisfied with the service I received. — Customer service
- I'm not satisfied with the quality of this product. — Retail complaint
- I'm not satisfied with the way this was handled. — Internal/professional

### Cultural Background

"I'm not satisfied" is a moderate, professional complaint phrase. Stronger: "This is unacceptable." Weaker: "I'm a bit disappointed."

## Could you fix this?

**Literal Translation:** Same.

**Meaning:** Polite request for repair/correction. Less accusatory than "This is broken."

**Usage Context:** Polite. Service counters, technical support, repair shops.

**Pattern:** Could you + fix + this?

### Examples

- Could you fix this? It stopped working yesterday. — Electronics repair
- Could you fix this before I leave? — Same-day request
- Could you fix this for free? It's still under warranty. — With condition

### Cultural Background

Asking "Could you fix this?" implies trust that the person can. The conditional "Could" softens what would otherwise be a command.

### Similar Expressions

- [[requests]] — Could you take a look?
- [[complaints]] — This isn't working.

## There's a problem with my order

**Literal Translation:** Same.

**Meaning:** Standard opening for a service complaint. Identifies the issue category without accusation.

**Usage Context:** Polite / neutral. Phone customer service, in-store service counters.

**Pattern:** There's a problem with + (my order / the system / etc.).

### Examples

- There's a problem with my order. It hasn't arrived yet. — Delivery complaint
- There's a problem with my bill. — Restaurant / hotel
- There's a problem with the app. — Tech support

### Cultural Background

"There's a problem with..." is a face-saving opener — it presents the issue as an objective fact rather than blaming the company. The agent can then investigate.

- [[complaints]] — This isn't what I ordered.
- [[complaints]] — I'd like to file a complaint.

## I'd like a refund

**Literal Translation:** Same.

**Meaning:** Clear, polite demand for money back. Standard consumer-rights phrase.

**Usage Context:** Polite but firm. Retail, online purchases, services.

**Pattern:** I'd like + a refund.

### Examples

- I'd like a refund, please. — Standard polite
- I'd like a refund, this product is defective. — With justification
- I'd like a refund or a replacement. — With alternative

### Cultural Background

"I'd like" is the polite conditional opener for any consumer demand. Direct "Give me a refund" is more confrontational.

### Similar Expressions

- [[requests]] — Could I get a refund?
- [[requests]] — I'd like to return this.

### Sources

- [[business-basics]]

## This is unacceptable

**Literal Translation:** Same.

**Meaning:** Strong, formal complaint. Signals serious dissatisfaction.

**Usage Context:** Formal / strong. Service failures, broken promises, repeated mistakes.

**Pattern:** This + is + unacceptable.

### Examples

- This is unacceptable. I've been waiting for two hours. — Restaurant
- This is unacceptable. The contract promised 24/7 support. — Business
- This is completely unacceptable. — Stronger emphasis

### Cultural Background

"This is unacceptable" is a clear escalation signal. It's the kind of phrase that triggers formal complaint procedures. Not for minor issues.

- [[complaints]] — I'm not satisfied with the service.
- [[emotions-reactions]] — This is outrageous.

## I'd like to file a complaint

**Literal Translation:** Same.

**Meaning:** Formal declaration that you're initiating a complaint procedure. Creates an official record.

**Usage Context:** Formal. Hotels, airlines, government offices, hospitals.

**Pattern:** I'd like to + file a complaint.

### Examples

- I'd like to file a complaint about the noise. — Apartment / hotel
- I'd like to file a formal complaint. — Strong emphasis
- I'd like to file a complaint with the manager. — Specific recipient

### Cultural Background

"Filing a complaint" is more formal than simply complaining — it creates documentation. Used when you want the issue on record or to escalate.

- [[requests]] — I'd like to speak with the manager.
- [[complaints]] — I'm not satisfied with the service.
