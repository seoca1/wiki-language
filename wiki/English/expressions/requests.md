# requests (English) expressions

> **Theme:** Daily Communication
> **Level:** A1-B1 (modal verbs + politeness)

Polite and direct ways to ask someone to do something. English requests lean heavily on modal verbs (can/could/would) and softening phrases ("I was wondering...", "Do you mind..."). Directness is calibrated to social distance: the closer the relationship, the less hedging is needed.

---

## Could you (help me)?

**Literal Translation:** "~해 주실 수 있나요?"

**Meaning:** A standard polite request using the past tense of "can" to soften the request.

**Usage Context:** Workplace, customer service, strangers, acquaintances. Safe default for most adult interactions.

**Pattern:** Could you + base verb + object? / Could you + base verb + for me?

### Examples

- Could you pass me the salt? (소금 좀 건네주시겠어요?)
- Could you send me the file by Friday? (그 파일 금요일까지 보내주실 수 있나요?)
- Could you speak a little more slowly, please? (좀 더 천천히 말씀해 주시겠어요?)

### Cultural Background

English speakers often choose past-tense modals (could/would) over present-tense ones (can/will) to sound softer and more formal. Pair with "please" for explicit politeness, and "Could you possibly..." adds another layer of hedging for larger favors.

## Would you mind doing...?

**Literal Translation:** "~하는 것이 마음에 걸리시나요?" (literal awkward; functionally: "~해 주시겠어요?")

**Meaning:** A very polite request, framed as if the listener might be inconvenienced.

**Usage Context:** Formal situations, requests to superiors, requests that take real effort.

**Pattern:** Would you mind + verb-ing? / Would you mind if I + past tense?

### Examples

- Would you mind opening the window? (창문 좀 열어주시겠어요?)
- Would you mind if I sat here? (여기 앉아도 될까요?)
- Would you mind helping me carry these boxes? (이 박스 들 좀 도와주시겠어요?)

### Cultural Background

This structure makes politeness visible: it explicitly acknowledges the listener's right to refuse. Note the unusual reply convention: "Yes, I would mind" means *no*, while "No, I don't mind" means *yes*. Learners often get this backward, so pair it with a confirmation ("Sure, go ahead").

## Do you mind if I...?

**Literal Translation:** "제가 ~해도 괜찮으시겠어요?"

**Meaning:** Asking permission for one's own action. The speaker is the subject of the proposed action.

**Usage Context:** Asking to enter, leave, interrupt, borrow, or change something that affects another person.

**Pattern:** Do you mind if I + present tense? / Do you mind my + verb-ing?

### Examples

- Do you mind if I open the door? It's hot in here. (문 좀 열어드려도 될까요? 여기 좀 더워서요.)
- Do you mind if I take a quick look at your notes? (노트 좀 잠깐 봐도 될까요?)
- Do you mind if we reschedule for next week? (다음 주로 일정 변경해도 괜찮으시겠어요?)

### Cultural Background

Same reply inversion as "Would you mind...": "Yes, I do mind" means *no*, "No, I don't mind" means *yes*. In American English many speakers collapse this to "Sure" / "Yeah, go ahead" without literal yes/no, which can confuse learners used to the strict inversion.

## Can I...?

**Literal Translation:** "제가 ~해도 될까요?"

**Meaning:** Direct, neutral request for permission. Slightly less formal than "Could I...?".

**Usage Context:** Everyday situations: ordering at a restaurant, asking to use the bathroom at a friend's house, requesting time off.

**Pattern:** Can I + base verb? / Can I have + noun?

### Examples

- Can I have a glass of water, please? (물 한 잔 주시겠어요?)
- Can I ask you something? (뭐 하나 여쭤봐도 될까요?)
- Can I get a coffee, please? (커피 한 잔 주세요.)

### Cultural Background

"Can I" is the everyday workhorse of English permission requests. "Can I get a coffee" is a common casual order at US coffee shops, though some style guides prefer "May I" or "Could I" for formal correctness. In spoken American English "Can I get..." is fully standard.

## May I...?

**Literal Translation:** "제가 ~해도 되겠습니까?"

**Meaning:** Formal request for permission. Considered the most grammatically correct of the permission modals in traditional usage.

**Usage Context:** Formal contexts: addressing elders, official meetings, writing formal emails, polite strangers.

**Pattern:** May I + base verb? / May I have + noun?

### Examples

- May I have your name, please? (성함을 여쭤봐도 될까요?)
- May I see your ID? (신분증 좀 보여주시겠습니까?)
- May I ask why you're leaving? ( 떠나시는 이유를 여쭤봐도 될까요?)

### Cultural Background

Prescriptive grammar traditions hold "may" for permission and "can" for ability, though in modern spoken English the two have largely merged. "May I" still sounds markedly more formal and is preferred in service scripts, official forms, and academic writing.

## I'd like to...

**Literal Translation:** "저는 ~하고 싶은데요."

**Meaning:** A polite way to state what you want, often when ordering, requesting, or proposing.

**Usage Context:** Restaurants, hotels, business meetings, formal requests where stating preference directly.

**Pattern:** I'd like + noun / I'd like to + base verb.

### Examples

- I'd like to make a reservation for two. (두 분 예약하려고요.)
- I'd like a cup of coffee, please. (커피 한 잔 주세요.)
- I'd like to speak to the manager, please. (매니저와 통화하고 싶습니다.)

### Cultural Background

"I'd like" softens "I want" — useful because direct "I want" can sound demanding in English. In customer-facing scripts (restaurants, hotels, airlines), "I'd like" is the standard polite phrasing.

## I was wondering if...

**Literal Translation:** "저는 ~인지 궁금했습니다." (literal); functionally: "~해도 될까요?"

**Meaning:** A heavily hedged request that pretends the speaker has been thinking about it for a while.

**Usage Context:** Email requests, asking supervisors for favors, requests that might be inconvenient.

**Pattern:** I was wondering if + subject + could / would + base verb.

### Examples

- I was wondering if you could help me with this report. (이 보고서 좀 도와주실 수 있을지 여�봤습니다.)
- I was wondering if we could move the meeting to Thursday. (목요일로 회의 옮겨도 될지 여쭤봤습니다.)
- I was wondering if you had any advice for me. (조언을 좀 구해도 될까 해서요.)

### Cultural Background

This phrase is a hallmark of professional English correspondence. It signals humility and gives the listener an easy out. Overusing it can sound evasive, but in business email it's near-universal for non-trivial asks.

## Excuse me, can I...?

**Literal Translation:** "실례합니다, ~해도 될까요?"

**Meaning:** A polite request that begins by signaling the speaker is about to interrupt or impose.

**Usage Context:** Approaching strangers, getting attention in a public place, politely entering a conversation.

**Pattern:** Excuse me, can I + base verb? / Excuse me + question clause.

### Examples

- Excuse me, can I get past? (실례합니다, 좀 지나가도 될까요?)
- Excuse me, could you tell me the time? (실례합니다, 지금 몇 시인지 알려주실 수 있나요?)
- Excuse me, do you know where the library is? (실례합니다, 도서관 어디 있는지 아시나요?)

### Cultural Background

"Excuse me" doubles as both an attention-getter and a soft apology for the interruption. Pairing it with a question makes the intrusion feel smaller. In American English, "excuse me" is also used to apologize for minor physical accidents, while "I'm sorry" is reserved for more serious social errors.

## Do you have a moment?

**Literal Translation:** "잠깐 시간 있으세요?"

**Meaning:** A soft way to ask for someone's time before making a request.

**Usage Context:** Approaching a busy colleague, opening a conversation with a stranger, asking for help.

**Pattern:** Do you have a moment? / Do you have a second? / Got a minute?

### Examples

- Do you have a moment? I need to ask you something. (잠깐 시간 있으세요? 여쭤볼 게 있어서요.)
- Got a minute? I want to run an idea by you. (1분만 돼요? 아이디어 하나 같이 봐주세요.)
- Do you have a second to look at this? (이거 잠깐 봐주실 수 있나요?)

### Cultural Background

This phrase acknowledges the listener's time as a resource. It implies "If you're busy, I'll go away" and so reduces the imposition. Variants like "Got a minute?" and "Got a sec?" are markedly more casual and common in American workplaces.

## I was hoping...

**Literal Translation:** "저는 ~을 바라고 있었습니다."

**Meaning:** A soft request that frames the speaker as having modest expectations.

**Usage Context:** Asking for help, requesting accommodations, asking for extensions on deadlines.

**Pattern:** I was hoping + subject + could / would + base verb. / I was hoping for + noun.

### Examples

- I was hoping you could send me the details. (자세한 내용 좀 보내주셨으면 해서요.)
- I was hoping for a few more days to finish this. (이거 마무리할 며칠 더 있었으면 해서요.)
- I was hoping we could talk about my schedule. (제 일정 관련해서 잠깐 이야기 나눌 수 있을까 해서요.)

### Cultural Background

"I was hoping" implies that the request has been on the speaker's mind — it personalizes the ask without making it sound entitled. Pairing it with "if possible" or "if it's not too much trouble" adds another layer of politeness, sometimes called "double-hedging."
