# Expressions: Idioms & Proverbs (English)

> **Theme:** Idioms & Proverbs
> **Level:** B2-C2
> **Standard:** US/UK English

English idioms and proverbs — common sayings and figurative expressions.

---

## Kill two birds with one stone

**Part of Speech:** idiom
**Definition:** "Kill two birds with one stone" — achieve two goals with one action.

### Usage

Describing efficiency.

### Cultural Notes

- **Origin**: Greek/Aesop's fables (much older)
- **Usage**: Universal in English (slightly violent imagery)
- **Variation**: "Two for one", "Twofer"
- **Modern alternative**: "Multitasking"

---

## Don't count your chickens before they hatch

**Part of Speech:** proverb
**Definition:** "Don't count your chickens before they hatch" — don't assume success too early.

### Usage

Warning against premature optimism.

### Cultural Notes

- **Origin**: Aesop's fables (Greek, ~600 BC)
- **Meaning**: Don't assume future success before it happens
- **Similar to**: "Don't put all eggs in one basket" (different meaning)
- **Modern use**: Stock market, project planning

---

## A penny saved is a penny earned

**Part of Speech:** proverb
**Definition:** "A penny saved is a penny earned" — saving money equals earning.

### Usage

Encouraging frugality.

### Cultural Notes

- **Origin**: Benjamin Franklin (1737, Poor Richard's Almanack)
- **Meaning**: Saving = earning (in terms of net worth)
- **Modern critique**: Inflation makes penny less valuable
- **Related**: "Take care of the pennies, the dollars take care of themselves"

---

## Don't put all your eggs in one basket

**Part of Speech:** proverb
**Definition:** "Don't put all your eggs in one basket" — diversify risk.

### Usage

Investment/business advice.

### Cultural Notes

- **Origin**: Miguel de Cervantes (Don Quixote, 1605)
- **Meaning**: Diversify investments/risks
- **Modern application**: Portfolio diversification, job skills, dating
- **Opposite**: "Double down" / "Go all in" (when confident)

---

## Rome wasn't built in a day

**Part of Speech:** proverb
**Definition:** "Rome wasn't built in a day" — major achievements take time.

### Usage

Encouraging patience with long projects.

### Cultural Notes

- **Origin**: Medieval French (12th century)
- **Meaning**: Important work takes time
- **Usage**: Learning a language, building a business, recovery
- **Related**: "Slow and steady wins the race"

---

## Actions speak louder than words

**Part of Speech:** proverb
**Definition:** "Actions speak louder than words" — what you do matters more than what you say.

### Usage

Criticizing empty promises.

### Cultural Notes

- **Origin**: St. Francis de Sales (17th century)
- **Meaning**: Behavior reveals true intent
- **Usage**: Relationships, business promises, politics
- **Related**: "Walk the talk"

---

## Better late than never

**Part of Speech:** proverb
**Definition:** "Better late than never" — doing something late is better than not at all.

### Usage

Acknowledging tardy completion.

### Cultural Notes

- **Origin**: Geoffrey Chaucer (Canterbury Tales, 1386)
- **Meaning**: Late action > no action
- **Caveat**: Doesn't excuse chronic lateness
- **Usage**: Apologies, late homework, late delivery

---

## Practice makes perfect

**Part of Speech:** proverb
**Definition:** "Practice makes perfect" — repetition leads to mastery.

### Usage

Encouraging consistent practice.

### Cultural Notes

- **Origin**: 1550s (English/Italian proverbs)
- **Meaning**: Skills improve with practice
- **Modern variation**: "Practice makes progress" (less pressure)
- **Usage**: Sports, music, language learning, work skills
